    How due dates are calculated

    Almost every due date in the world starts with a piece of arithmetic from the early 1800s called Naegele's rule. Take the first day of your last menstrual period, add seven days, subtract three months, and add a year — and you have the date a baby is “due” if the pregnancy lasts exactly 40 weeks. In plain day-counting terms, the rule simply says: due date = first day of your last period + 280 days. That is the calculation running behind the LMP option above, and it is the same method used on the paper pregnancy wheels you may see spinning on a midwife's desk.

    The 40 weeks are counted from your last period rather than from conception for a practical reason: most people can remember (or look up) the day their period started, while the exact moment of conception is almost never known. Conception usually happens about two weeks after that period begins, around ovulation, which is why a “40-week” pregnancy is really about 38 weeks of actual baby-growing. When you do know your conception date — through fertility treatment, ovulation testing or careful tracking — the conception option above adds 266 days instead, and both methods should land within a day or two of each other. If you would like the full story, including why cycle length matters and where the rule came from, our guide How Is a Due Date Calculated? Naegele's Rule Explained walks through it step by step.

    The cycle-length slider is there because Naegele's rule quietly assumes a textbook 28-day cycle with ovulation on day 14. Real cycles are rarely textbook. If your cycle is usually 35 days, you probably ovulate around day 21 rather than day 14, so your baby has had about a week less growing time than the standard rule assumes — and your due date should move about a week later. A 24-day cycle does the opposite. Adjusting for cycle length is the single easiest way to make an LMP-based estimate more honest, although it still cannot beat an early scan, as we explain in LMP vs Ultrasound Dating: Which Is More Accurate?

    Why only about 5% of babies arrive on their due date

    Here is the fact that surprises almost everyone: the due date is not a deadline, and it was never meant to be. Studies of hundreds of thousands of births consistently find that only about one baby in twenty — roughly 5% — is actually born on the estimated due date. The date is better understood as the centre of a window. A baby is considered “full term” anywhere from 37 completed weeks to 42 weeks, and the large majority of babies arrive somewhere inside that five-week span, most of them in the week either side of the due date itself.

    There are good biological reasons for the spread. Ovulation does not happen on a metronome, even in people with regular cycles, so the true starting point of any pregnancy wobbles by several days. Implantation timing varies too. First babies tend to gestate a little longer than subsequent ones, and genetics plays a quiet role — the length of your own mother's pregnancies is a surprisingly decent hint about yours. Add in the fact that the 280-day figure is itself an average rounded for convenience, and it becomes clear that the due date is a well-informed estimate sitting in the middle of a natural range, not an appointment your baby has agreed to keep.

    This is worth knowing emotionally as much as medically. The final weeks feel long, well-meaning friends start texting “any news?” from about week 38, and it is easy to feel as though something is wrong when the date passes quietly. In reality, passing your due date is one of the most ordinary things in pregnancy — roughly half of first-time mothers are still pregnant on the day itself. Our guide What Happens If You Go Past Your Due Date? explains what monitoring and induction discussions usually look like after 40 weeks, and why most care teams are relaxed until 41–42 weeks.

    What an ultrasound dating scan changes

    At your first ultrasound — often called the dating scan, usually offered between about 8 and 14 weeks — the sonographer measures the baby from crown to rump. In the first trimester, embryos grow at a remarkably consistent rate, so that single measurement can date a pregnancy to within about five to seven days. If the scan-based date differs from your LMP date by more than a few days, most providers will adopt the scan date as your official estimated due date, and it will not change again for the rest of the pregnancy, even if later scans suggest the baby is measuring big or small.

    That last point matters: after the first trimester, babies grow at individual rates, just as children do, so a later scan showing a “big” baby does not mean the due date was wrong — it usually just means you are growing a bigger baby. This is exactly why dating is done early and then left alone. If your date does change at the scan, do not panic: it most often means you ovulated a little earlier or later than day 14, which is completely normal. We compare the two methods in depth — accuracy, margins of error, and when each one wins — in LMP vs Ultrasound Dating: Which Is More Accurate?

    What to do week by week, at a high level

    A due date turns an abstract pregnancy into a timeline you can plan around. Below is the shape of those 40 weeks in broad strokes — the appointments and decisions that typically fall in each stretch. It is a map, not a schedule: your own care team will set your actual appointments, and our First Trimester Checklist: Weeks 1 to 12 covers the opening stretch in much more detail.

    Weeks 1–4: before you know

    Oddly, the first two “weeks” of pregnancy happen before conception: week 1 is the week of your period, and conception usually occurs near the end of week 2. By week 4, a home test can usually detect the pregnancy hormone hCG. If you are trying to conceive, this is the window where folic acid, avoiding alcohol, and reviewing any medications with a doctor matter most — ideally started before you ever see a positive test.

    Weeks 5–8: the first appointment

    The embryo's heart begins to beat around week 6, and many people book their first prenatal appointment in this window. Expect blood tests, a review of your health history, and early conversations about screening options. Nausea and exhaustion often arrive now; both are usually at their worst before they get better.

    Weeks 9–13: the dating scan

    This is when the dating ultrasound typically happens and your due date may be confirmed or adjusted. Optional screening tests for chromosomal conditions are also offered in this window in many countries. By week 13 the baby has fingers, toes and the beginnings of fingerprints, and the risk of miscarriage drops substantially — which is why many people choose the end of this stretch to share their news.

    Weeks 14–20: the second trimester begins

    Energy often returns, nausea fades, and the bump starts to show. The detailed anatomy scan, usually around 18–20 weeks, checks the baby's organs, limbs, placenta position and growth — and can often reveal the sex if you want to know. Many people feel the first flutters of movement somewhere in this stretch, earlier if it is not their first baby.

    Weeks 21–27: movement and the glucose test

    Kicks become unmistakable and start to form patterns. Between roughly 24 and 28 weeks most providers offer a glucose screening test for gestational diabetes, along with blood checks for anaemia. The baby's lungs and hearing are developing rapidly, and from around 24 weeks the baby is considered potentially viable outside the womb with intensive support — a quiet milestone many parents find reassuring.

    Weeks 28–36: the third trimester

    Appointments become more frequent. This is the season of growth spurts (the baby's and yours), Braxton Hicks practice contractions, antenatal classes, maternity-leave paperwork, and practical decisions: where to give birth, what to pack, how the baby will sleep and travel. Vaccinations such as whooping cough and flu are commonly offered in this window, depending on your country's schedule.

    Weeks 37–40: full term

    From 37 weeks the baby is considered early term, and from 39 weeks, full term. The baby usually settles head-down. Appointments now focus on position, blood pressure and signs that labour is near. Most babies arrive in this stretch — and, as the statistics above show, plenty politely ignore the date in the middle of it.

    Weeks 41–42: past the date

    If you are still pregnant at 41 weeks, expect extra monitoring — heartbeat traces and ultrasound checks of fluid and the placenta — and a conversation about induction, which most guidelines suggest offering by 41–42 weeks. Our guide on going past your due date covers what to expect, what the monitoring involves, and the questions worth asking.

    How Is a Due Date Calculated? Naegele's Rule Explained

    Almost every due date ever given starts with a rule written down in the early nineteenth century. Here is how it works, why it counts from your last period rather than from conception, and where its limits are.

    The rule itself

    Naegele's rule — named after Franz Karl Naegele, a German obstetrician who popularised it in the early 1800s — gives you a due date with three steps: take the first day of your last menstrual period, add seven days, subtract three months, and then add one year. If your last period started on 10 January, adding seven days gives 17 January; subtracting three months takes you back to 17 October of the previous year; adding a year brings you to 17 October of this year. That is the estimated due date.

    Behind the calendar juggling sits a simpler fact: the rule assumes a pregnancy lasts 280 days — exactly 40 weeks — from the first day of the last period. The “plus seven, minus three months” version is just a shortcut that lands on roughly the same date without counting out 280 individual days. It is the same calculation our due date calculator performs when you choose the last-period option, and the same one printed on the cardboard pregnancy wheels still found in clinics everywhere.

    Why count from the last period at all?

    It seems backwards: the pregnancy is dated from roughly two weeks before the baby even exists. The reason is purely practical. The first day of a period is an event most people notice and can date with reasonable confidence. Conception, by contrast, is invisible. Sperm can survive for up to about five days, an egg for roughly a day, and fertilisation leaves no sensation at all — so even people who know exactly when they had intercourse rarely know the precise day a pregnancy began. A dating system needs a starting gun everyone can hear, and the last period was the best available.

    The consequence is a small quirk of language you will meet everywhere in pregnancy: you are described as “four weeks pregnant” at the moment a test first turns positive, even though the embryo is about two weeks old. Doctors call this gestational age, and it is the number used for every scan measurement, screening window and milestone in this site's guides. When people instead count from conception, they are describing embryonic age — always about two weeks less.

    The assumption hiding inside the rule

    Naegele's rule only produces the right answer if two things are true: that your cycle is 28 days long, and that you ovulate on day 14. For a large minority of people, one or both are false — and the rule has no way of knowing. A person with a 35-day cycle probably ovulated around day 21, a week “late” by the rule's standards, so a rule-based due date will be about a week too early. A 24-day cycle flips the error the other way. This is why our calculator includes a cycle-length adjustment: every day your cycle differs from 28 shifts the due date by a day in the same direction. It is also the entire subject of our guide for readers whose cycles refuse to follow the pattern at all: What If My Cycle Is Irregular? Due Dates Explained.

    The other ways professionals estimate a due date

    Conception date. If conception is known precisely — most often with IVF or other fertility treatment, sometimes with meticulous ovulation tracking — the due date is simply 266 days (38 weeks) after conception. Our calculator offers this option too. With IVF, dating is even more exact: the embryo's age at transfer is known to the day, which is why fertility clinics quote due dates with unusual confidence.

    Ultrasound measurement. An early scan measures the embryo's crown–rump length and converts it into an age. Because embryos grow at a very consistent rate in the first trimester, this is generally the most accurate method of all, with a margin of roughly five to seven days. When it disagrees with the period-based date by more than a few days, most care teams adopt the scan date. We compare the two methods head-to-head in LMP vs Ultrasound Dating: Which Is More Accurate?

    Clinical examination and later landmarks. Before ultrasound, clinicians estimated dates from when the uterus could first be felt, when the heartbeat could first be heard, and when the mother first felt movement (quickening — around 18–20 weeks in a first pregnancy). These methods are rough, with margins of several weeks, and are now mostly a backup where scans are unavailable.

    How accurate is any of this?

    However it is calculated, a due date is the centre of a distribution, not a prediction. Only about 5% of babies arrive on the exact day; the overwhelming majority arrive within the fortnight either side, and “term” officially spans 37 to 42 weeks. Even the 280-day figure is a rounded average — research on spontaneous labours suggests the true average for a first baby is a few days beyond 40 weeks. None of this makes the due date useless: it schedules scans and screening tests, tracks growth, and tells clinicians when a pregnancy has run long enough to need extra watching. It just means the date deserves a pencil, not a pen. If your date comes and goes, our guide What Happens If You Go Past Your Due Date? explains what happens next.

    LMP vs Ultrasound Dating: Which Is More Accurate?

    You calculated one due date from your last period, and then the scan suggested another. Which date is right, why did they disagree, and which one will your care team actually use?

    Two methods, two different kinds of information

    The LMP method — dating from the first day of your last menstrual period, as described in our guide to how a due date is calculated — is really a prediction about your cycle. It assumes your period date is remembered correctly, that your cycle runs 28 days, and that you ovulated on day 14. When all three hold, it is excellent. When any of them slips, the date slips with it, and the method itself cannot tell you that anything went wrong.

    Ultrasound dating works from the baby rather than the calendar. In the first trimester, the sonographer measures the embryo from the top of its head to its bottom — the crown–rump length — and matches that measurement against growth charts built from thousands of pregnancies with known conception dates. Early embryos are strikingly uniform: at eight weeks, healthy embryos measure within a millimetre or two of each other regardless of their parents' size. That uniformity is what makes the method powerful.

    The accuracy numbers

    A first-trimester crown–rump measurement dates a pregnancy to within roughly five to seven days. An LMP date, in a person with regular 28-day cycles and a certain memory, is comparable — but studies repeatedly find that a substantial share of people are unsure of their period dates, misremember them, or ovulated earlier or later than day 14, and in real-world populations LMP dating is off by more than a week considerably more often than scan dating. Scans performed later are less precise: a second-trimester measurement carries a margin of about one to two weeks, and a third-trimester scan can be out by three weeks or more, because by then babies genuinely come in different sizes.

    Why your scan date differs from your period date

    The most common reason is also the most reassuring: you ovulated on a different day than the textbook assumes. A scan date five days later than your LMP date usually means conception happened around day 19 instead of day 14 — unremarkable, especially if your cycles run long. A scan date earlier than expected suggests early ovulation. Other ordinary causes include a misremembered period (implantation bleeding mistaken for a light period is a classic), a cycle disrupted by recent contraception, stress, illness or travel, and simple rounding. Rarely, a large discrepancy prompts the care team to look more carefully at growth — one more reason the comparison itself is useful information rather than a problem.

    Which date wins?

    In most modern guidelines, the rule is straightforward: if an early ultrasound (ideally before 14 weeks) disagrees with the LMP date by more than about five to seven days, the ultrasound date becomes the official estimated due date, because it is based on the baby's actual size at the stage when size tracks age most tightly. If the two agree within that margin, many providers keep the LMP date. Either way, once the official date is set in the first trimester, it is essentially never revised again.

    That “never revised” part surprises people later in pregnancy. At the 30-week scan your baby might measure two weeks ahead, and the report will not move your due date — nor should it. Later measurements reflect how big your baby is growing, not how old the pregnancy is, and big babies are not necessarily older babies. Changing the date late in pregnancy would corrupt every growth assessment that depends on it.

    What this means for you practically

    Use the LMP calculation — including the cycle-length adjustment on our calculator — as your working estimate until the dating scan, especially for booking that first appointment. Then accept the scan date as the official one, even if it moves things by a few days, and let it settle every downstream decision: screening windows, the anatomy scan at 18–20 weeks, and, at the far end, conversations about induction if you go past your due date. If your cycles are irregular, the scan matters even more for you, as we explain in our irregular-cycle guide — for some readers it is not a refinement of the LMP date but a replacement for one that was never reliable in the first place.

    What If My Cycle Is Irregular? Due Dates Explained

    Naegele's rule assumes a tidy 28-day cycle. If yours is 24 days one month and 40 the next — or you have PCOS, have just stopped contraception, or conceived while breastfeeding — here is how due dates work for you.

    Why irregular cycles break the standard formula

    Every period-based due date calculation rests on one hidden assumption: that you ovulated about 14 days after your period started. In reality, what stays roughly constant is the second half of the cycle — the luteal phase between ovulation and the next period, usually about 12–14 days. The first half is the flexible part. A 35-day cycle almost always means ovulation around day 21, not a longer luteal phase. So when the standard rule counts 280 days from your period, it quietly gives your baby credit for a week of growth that had not happened yet, and your “due date” lands about a week too early. With cycles that vary from month to month, there is no way to know which version of your cycle conceived — and the error can run to two or three weeks.

    Step one: use what you know about your own pattern

    If your cycles are consistently long or consistently short, you are not really “irregular” — you are regular at a different length, and the fix is easy. Set the cycle-length slider on our due date calculator to your typical length; the estimate shifts day-for-day with your likely ovulation. If your cycles vary but you tracked ovulation — with test strips, basal body temperature, cervical mucus, or a fertility app backed by real symptoms — you may know your conception window within a day or two, and the calculator's conception-date option will serve you better than any period-based guess.

    Common situations, one by one

    Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)

    With PCOS, ovulation may happen only a few times a year, at unpredictable points in long cycles. An LMP date can be off by many weeks — a positive test “six weeks” after a period might mean a genuinely six-week pregnancy or a three-week one. Early ultrasound is not a luxury here; it is the only reliable dating method, and it is worth asking for one promptly rather than waiting for the standard 12-week appointment.

    Just stopped hormonal contraception

    The first bleed after stopping the pill is often a withdrawal bleed, not a true period, and the first real cycle afterwards can be longer than usual while ovulation restarts. Dating from that bleed can mislead by one to three weeks. Many providers recommend either waiting for one natural period before trying, or simply relying on an early scan for dating if you conceive straight away.

    Breastfeeding or recently postpartum

    It is entirely possible to ovulate — and conceive — before your first postpartum period ever returns, which means there may be no LMP to calculate from at all. In that case the dating scan does all the work, and the conception-date method is useless unless you happen to know the day. This is common, and maternity services handle it routinely.

    Perimenopause and very variable cycles

    When cycles swing between three weeks and three months, the LMP method produces a number with no real meaning. Treat any calculator result as a placeholder for booking care, and expect the scan to replace it.

    The dating scan is your answer

    For irregular cycles, the first-trimester ultrasound is not a tiebreaker — it is the method. A crown–rump measurement between about 8 and 13 weeks dates the pregnancy within roughly five to seven days no matter what your cycle did, because it measures the baby instead of inferring dates from your hormones. Our comparison of LMP vs ultrasound dating explains the margins in detail. Practical advice: tell your provider at booking that your cycles are irregular and you are unsure of your dates. In most systems that is a recognised reason to arrange the scan at the earlier end of the window, and it protects the accuracy of time-sensitive screening tests that depend on knowing your exact week.

    What an inaccurate early estimate can affect

    A wrong due date is more than an inconvenience. Screening tests in the first trimester are only valid inside narrow gestational windows. Growth scans later in pregnancy judge your baby against the due date — a date set two weeks too early can make a healthy baby look growth-restricted, while one set too late can mask the opposite. And at the end of pregnancy, induction decisions hinge on the date: nobody wants labour induced at “41 weeks” that is really 39. Getting dated early and accurately — by scan, if your cycle cannot be trusted — prevents the whole chain of small errors. Until then, use our calculator with your best information, hold the result lightly, and read our First Trimester Checklist for what else the early weeks involve.

    First Trimester Checklist: Weeks 1 to 12

    The first twelve weeks are a strange mix of secrecy, symptoms and appointments. This checklist walks through what typically happens, what to book, what to start (and stop), and which symptoms should never wait.

    Weeks 1–4: the groundwork

    Weeks 1 and 2 are counted before conception — week 1 begins with your period — and fertilisation usually happens near the end of week 2. Most people feel nothing at all in this stretch. If you are trying to conceive, the important jobs are already familiar: take folic acid (typically 400 micrograms daily, started at least a month before conception where possible, because the baby's neural tube forms by week 6 — before many people know they are pregnant), stop smoking and alcohol, and ask a doctor or pharmacist to review any regular medications, since a few common ones are best swapped before pregnancy. By the end of week 4, a home test can usually confirm the pregnancy.

    Weeks 5–8: book in and settle in

    Book your first prenatal appointment. In most systems you contact your doctor, midwife service or obstetrician soon after a positive test; the first full “booking” appointment often happens around weeks 8–10, though an earlier visit may be arranged if you have a medical condition, a previous ectopic pregnancy, or very uncertain dates (see our guide on irregular cycles and due dates). Expect the booking visit to cover your health history, blood pressure, blood tests — blood group, anaemia, immunity and infection screening — and a conversation about screening options for chromosomal conditions, which must be scheduled inside specific windows.

    Expect symptoms. Nausea (at any hour, despite the name “morning sickness”), profound tiredness, sore breasts, a heightened sense of smell and frequent trips to the bathroom are all typical from around week 6. Small, frequent, bland meals and ginger help some people; rest helps most. Eating little and often is a reasonable strategy — this is not the trimester to worry about diet perfection.

    Keep taking folic acid through week 12. Ask your provider whether you also need vitamin D or a higher folate dose (recommended for some people, including those with diabetes or a family history of neural tube defects). Check which foods your local guidance advises avoiding — typically unpasteurised cheeses, raw or undercooked eggs and meat, certain fish high in mercury, and liver products.

    Weeks 9–12: the dating scan and screening

    The dating ultrasound, usually between weeks 8 and 14, is the highlight of the trimester for most people: the first sight of the baby, a heartbeat, and a confirmed or revised due date. As we explain in LMP vs Ultrasound Dating, a measurement at this stage is accurate to within about a week, and if it disagrees with your period dates, the scan date usually becomes the official one — the same date our calculator estimated, refined.

    Screening decisions also land in this window. Depending on where you live you may be offered a combined first-trimester screening (a blood test plus a nuchal translucency measurement at the scan), or non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) from a maternal blood sample, typically from week 10. These are screening tests — they estimate probability rather than giving a diagnosis — and they are entirely optional. It is worth reading about them before the appointment rather than deciding in the corridor.

    Practical jobs for this stretch: tell your employer only when you are ready (many people wait until after the 12-week scan, though earlier disclosure can help if your job involves heavy lifting, chemicals or long shifts); check what maternity benefits and leave you are entitled to; if you have dental work pending, pregnancy is a good reason to get it done — gum problems are common in pregnancy; and if you plan to use childcare, believe it or not, popular nurseries in some cities genuinely do fill lists this early.

    Symptoms that always deserve a same-day call

    Most first-trimester discomfort is normal, but some symptoms should always be checked the same day: vaginal bleeding, especially with pain; sharp or one-sided pelvic pain; pain in the tip of the shoulder; severe vomiting where you cannot keep fluids down for 24 hours; fainting or severe dizziness; fever above 38°C (100.4°F); or pain when urinating. These can signal ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage, dehydration or infection — all things your care team would much rather rule out early than hear about late. Light spotting is common and often harmless, but it still earns a call; that is what the phone line is for.

    By the end of week 12

    You should have: a confirmed due date, a booking appointment completed, screening decided and done or scheduled, folic acid under way, and the first scan picture in a drawer somewhere. The risk of miscarriage falls sharply after this point, nausea usually begins to ease, and many people start sharing their news. The second trimester — energy returning, the anatomy scan, first felt movements — is a different country. Our homepage guide to what happens week by week sketches the rest of the journey, and if you are counting ahead to the finish line, What Happens If You Go Past Your Due Date? covers the final chapter.

    What Happens If You Go Past Your Due Date?

    The date is circled, the bag is packed, the texts are arriving — and nothing is happening. Going past your due date is one of the most common experiences in pregnancy. Here is what it means, what your care team will do, and how induction actually works.

    First: you are not late yet

    The language matters. A pregnancy is “term” from 37 weeks, “full term” from 39, and only becomes post-term at 42 completed weeks. The day after your due date you are not overdue in any medical sense — you are at 40 weeks and one day, well inside the normal window. As our guide to how due dates are calculated explains, the date is the midpoint of a five-week term window, and only about 5% of babies arrive on it. Around half of first-time mothers are still pregnant on the day itself, and roughly one in ten pregnancies continues past 41 weeks. Statistically, you are in very large, very ordinary company.

    Why some pregnancies run long

    Sometimes the dates were simply a little off — ovulation a few days later than assumed, a long cycle quietly stretching the pregnancy (the situation our irregular cycle guide covers). Sometimes the baby is a first baby; first labours tend to start later. Genetics plays a part: if your mother went to 42 weeks, you are more likely to as well. And sometimes there is no reason at all beyond normal human variation — the same variation that makes some babies arrive at 38 weeks with no explanation. A genuinely post-term pregnancy is not caused by anything you did or failed to do: not by resting too much, walking too little, or skipping the spicy food.

    What monitoring looks like after 40 weeks

    Care ramps up gently. Appointments may become twice-weekly after 41 weeks, typically including checks of your blood pressure and urine, measurement of the bump, and listening to or electronically monitoring the baby's heartbeat (a CTG trace). Many providers also offer an ultrasound to check the amniotic fluid level and the placenta's condition, since the placenta gradually becomes less efficient after term and fluid tends to decline. You will usually be asked to pay attention to the baby's movements: a clear reduction in movement at any point in this period is a reason to call the same day, not to wait for the next appointment. At each visit you can also expect a conversation — not a verdict — about induction: its timing, its methods, and your preferences.

    The membrane sweep

    Often the first step offered, sometimes from 40–41 weeks, is a membrane sweep (or “stretch and sweep”): during a vaginal examination, the midwife or doctor sweeps a finger around the cervix to separate the membranes from the lower womb, which can release prostaglandins that encourage labour. It takes a minute, is commonly described as uncomfortable rather than painful, and modestly increases the chance of labour starting within the next few days. It can be repeated, and declining one is always an option.

    How induction works

    Most guidelines suggest offering induction somewhere between 41 and 42 weeks, because the small risks associated with a post-term pregnancy — chiefly a gradually ageing placenta and a slowly rising stillbirth risk — begin to outweigh the benefits of waiting. Induction is a process, not a single event, and it can take days. Typically it begins with ripening the cervix, using a prostaglandin pessary or gel, or a small balloon catheter, often over 24 hours. If the cervix opens enough, the waters may be broken artificially. If contractions do not follow, a hormone drip (synthetic oxytocin) is started and increased gradually. At every stage there are decision points, and you can ask what happens if you wait, what the alternatives are, and what your care team recommends for your specific situation. Induction does not automatically mean a caesarean — most induced labours end in a vaginal birth — though induced contractions are often described as more intense, and continuous monitoring is common.

    Coping with the wait

    The last stretch is as much psychological as physical. Practical defences help: give well-wishers a script (“we'll share news when there is news — the window runs to mid-month”), put the phone on silent without guilt, and make small plans a day or two ahead so each day has a shape. Rest properly rather than marching up hills in the hope of triggering something; the evidence for walking, curry, pineapple and the rest is thin, and exhaustion is a poor way to start labour. Keep eating, keep hydrated, keep the hospital bag by the door — and remind yourself of the arithmetic from the start: your baby is not late, your baby is simply taking the normal amount of time, at the slower end of normal. If anxiety is building, say so at your next appointment; that conversation is part of care too.     This article is general information, not medical advice. Post-term care varies between providers; follow your own care team's plan, and always call promptly about reduced fetal movements, bleeding, or leaking fluid.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Quick answers to the questions we hear most. For the longer stories, follow the links into the guides.

    How accurate is this due date calculator?

    It uses the same standard method (Naegele's rule) used in clinics, so it is as accurate as the information you give it. If your period date is right and your cycle is regular, expect the true date to fall within about a week either side. Only around 5% of babies are born on the exact due date, so treat the result as the centre of a window, not a deadline. An early ultrasound is the most accurate way to date a pregnancy — see LMP vs Ultrasound Dating.

    What is Naegele's rule?

    It is the classic formula for estimating a due date: take the first day of your last period, add seven days, subtract three months and add a year. In plain terms it says pregnancy lasts 280 days (40 weeks) from the first day of your last period. Our guide How Is a Due Date Calculated? explains it fully, including the assumptions hiding inside it.

    Why does the calculator ask for my cycle length?

    Because the standard rule assumes a 28-day cycle with ovulation on day 14. If your cycle is usually 35 days, you likely ovulate around day 21, so your due date should be about a week later than the standard rule suggests. Adjusting the cycle length moves the estimate by exactly that difference. If your cycles are all over the place, read What If My Cycle Is Irregular?

    Should I use the LMP method or the conception-date method?

    Use whichever date you know more confidently. Most people use the LMP method because a period start is easier to remember. If you know your conception date precisely — for example through fertility treatment or ovulation tracking — the conception method (due date = conception + 266 days) is at least as good, and the two methods should agree within a couple of days.

    My scan gave a different due date. Which one is right?

    Probably the scan. First-trimester ultrasound measures the baby at the stage when all babies grow at nearly the same rate, so it dates a pregnancy to within about five to seven days. If the scan date differs from your period date by more than a few days, most providers adopt the scan date as official — usually it just means you ovulated earlier or later than day 14. See LMP vs Ultrasound Dating: Which Is More Accurate?

    How many weeks pregnant am I?

    Run the calculator — it shows your current week and day (for example, “12 weeks, 3 days”) based on today's date, along with your trimester and how many days remain. Pregnancy weeks are counted from the first day of your last period, which is why you are “four weeks pregnant” around the time of a positive test.

    When does each trimester start and end?

    The first trimester runs from week 1 to the end of week 13. The second trimester covers weeks 14 to 27. The third trimester starts at week 28 and continues until the birth. The calculator tells you which trimester you are in today and lists the dates each stage begins in your milestone list.

    What if I don't know the date of my last period?

    You are far from alone — it is one of the most common situations in early pregnancy care. Book your first appointment anyway and tell them your dates are uncertain; an early dating ultrasound can establish the pregnancy's age directly from the baby's measurements, no period date required. If you conceived while breastfeeding or just after stopping contraception, our irregular cycle guide covers your situation.

    I'm past my due date. Should I be worried?

    In most cases, no — it is extremely common, and “post-term” does not begin until 42 weeks. After 41 weeks your care team will usually offer extra monitoring and discuss induction. Keep an eye on your baby's movements and call the same day if they reduce noticeably. Our guide What Happens If You Go Past Your Due Date? walks through what to expect.
